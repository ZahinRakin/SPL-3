CASE REPORT — CORPORATE FINANCE / BANKRUPTCY
Case Reference: In re Cascade Retail Group, Inc. — Chapter 11 Bankruptcy
Case Number: BK-2023-40017
Court: U.S. Bankruptcy Court, District of Delaware
Filed: March 15, 2023
Status: Plan Confirmed — November 8, 2023; Emergence from Bankruptcy — January 15, 2024

=============================================================================
CASE OVERVIEW
=============================================================================
Cascade Retail Group, Inc. ("Cascade" or "Debtor") is a mid-size specialty retail chain operating 284 stores across 38 states, primarily selling home goods, kitchenware, and seasonal merchandise under the "Cascade Home" and "Seasons & More" banners. After experiencing severe financial distress driven by rising inventory costs, an e-commerce transformation failure, post-pandemic consumer spending normalization, and an over-leveraged balance sheet from a 2019 private equity acquisition, Cascade filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection seeking to restructure $1.6 billion in funded debt.

The case represents one of the more complex retail restructurings of 2023, involving 284 store leases, 4,800 employees, 1,200+ vendor relationships, and a contested reorganization plan.

=============================================================================
CAUSES OF FINANCIAL DISTRESS
=============================================================================

1. Leveraged Buyout Debt Burden:
   Apollo acquired Cascade in 2019 for $2.4 billion, using $1.3 billion in borrowed funds. The resulting debt service obligations consumed 35% of EBITDA in a normalized business environment — leaving virtually no cushion for economic disruption.

2. Failed E-Commerce Initiative:
   Cascade launched a $180 million technology investment program in 2020 to build direct-to-consumer e-commerce capabilities to compete with Amazon and Wayfair. The initiative launched 18 months late, over budget, and with significant customer experience failures. Online sales never exceeded 8% of total revenue vs. a target of 25%.

3. Post-COVID Consumer Normalization:
   The home goods category experienced extraordinary COVID-era demand (2020-2021) as consumers invested in their living spaces. Cascade dramatically increased inventory commitments based on this elevated demand. When consumer spending normalized and shifted toward experiences and services in 2022, Cascade was left with $320 million in excess inventory, driving margin compression and requiring deep discounting.

4. Inflation and Supply Chain:
   Rising freight costs (+42% YoY in 2022), inventory carrying costs, and store operating expense inflation compressed gross margins from 44% (2021) to 31% (2022) and EBITDA from $280 million (2021) to $48 million (2022).

5. Lease Obligations:
   284 stores under long-term leases signed in 2018-2019 at peak retail rents became significant fixed cost burdens as several locations underperformed. Lease rejection or renegotiation was a key restructuring tool.

=============================================================================
CHAPTER 11 PROCEEDINGS
=============================================================================

First Day Motions (March 15-16, 2023):
  Court approved on an emergency basis:
  - DIP (Debtor-in-Possession) Financing: $300 million DIP facility (Agent: First National Bank; Senior Lenders participating)
    Terms: SOFR + 600 bps; 12-month term; priming lien over pre-petition first lien debt
    Purpose: Fund ongoing operations, vendor payments, employee wages during restructuring
  - Critical Vendor Program: $45 million authorized to pay pre-petition claims of critical vendors (those providing unique merchandise or logistics services not readily replaceable)
  - Customer Loyalty Program: Continuation of Cascade Rewards loyalty program to maintain customer relationships
  - Employee Retention Plan: Retention bonuses for 340 key management and operational employees

Lease Decisions:
  Over the first 90 days, Cascade analyzed all 284 store leases using store-level profitability analysis. Results:
  - 198 stores: profitable or breakeven — RETAIN (some with rent concessions from landlords)
  - 62 stores: underperforming — CLOSE AND REJECT LEASE
  - 24 stores: viable with rent reduction negotiated — ASSUME WITH MODIFICATION

  Lease rejection resulted in landlord rejection damage claims of approximately $180 million (subject to the statutory cap under 11 U.S.C. Section 502(b)(6) — capped at 15% of remaining rent obligation, reduced total to approximately $42 million in allowable claims).

  Store closure sale-on-premises: 62 closing stores' inventory liquidated through going-out-of-business sales generating $48 million in proceeds.

Vendor Negotiations:
  Cascade negotiated revised payment terms with key merchandise suppliers:
  - Pre-petition arrears: 30-50 cents on the dollar
  - Post-petition trade credit: Restored on 30-day terms after partial payment
  - 18 vendors elected to provide trade credit in exchange for enhanced claim treatment in the plan

Committee Formation (March 28, 2023):
  U.S. Trustee appointed:
  (1) Official Committee of Unsecured Creditors (UCC): 7 members including top merchandise vendors, commercial landlords, and a bondholder representative
  (2) Retiree Committee: Not required (no formal retiree benefit obligations)

  UCC retained Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld as counsel and Ankura Consulting as financial advisor.

Sale Process vs. Reorganization Decision:
  First Lien Lenders initially pushed for a Section 363 asset sale process as the value-maximizing alternative. Debtor and Second Lien Noteholders advocated for a standalone reorganization. After 60 days of marketing by investment bank Lazard, the process yielded no qualified bids exceeding recovery values of a reorganization plan. Standalone reorganization pursued.

=============================================================================
PLAN OF REORGANIZATION
=============================================================================

Proposed Treatment of Claims:

  Class 1 — DIP Financing ($300M): Paid in full in cash at emergence (first priority)
  Class 2 — First Lien Secured Claims ($962M including fees and accrued interest):
    Treatment: $500M in new first lien exit facility (5-year term, SOFR + 400 bps) + 72% of new common equity
    Recovery: Approximately 78-84 cents on the dollar (blended)

  Class 3 — Second Lien Secured Claims ($350M):
    Treatment: $50M cash + 25% of new common equity + warrants for additional 8%
    Recovery: Approximately 20-22 cents on the dollar

  Class 4 — General Unsecured Claims ($380M including vendor claims and rejected lease claims):
    Treatment: 3% of new common equity + rights offering participation (pro rata)
    Recovery: Approximately 3-5 cents on the dollar

  Class 5 — Existing Equity (Apollo, public shareholders):
    Treatment: CANCELLED AND EXTINGUISHED
    Recovery: Zero

Valuation:
  Reorganized enterprise value: $780-920 million (based on DCF and EBITDA multiples; 3.8-4.5x projected FY2025 EBITDA of $175M-$205M)

Rights Offering:
  $75 million equity rights offering backstopped by Second Lien Noteholders consortium; allows unsecured creditors to purchase new equity at plan value

Plan Confirmation Objections:
  - Apollo (equity holder): Challenged enterprise valuation as too low; argued equity has value
  - Several landlords: Challenged lease rejection damage caps
  - UCC: Objected to Second Lien treatment as too generous relative to unsecured creditors
  - Judge overruled all objections; plan confirmed November 8, 2023

=============================================================================
ANALYSIS AND LESSONS LEARNED
=============================================================================

1. LBO Leverage Risk: The Apollo LBO placed a struggling retailer with intensifying competitive dynamics under an unsustainable debt load. Retail is a cyclical, margin-compressed industry ill-suited for high leverage ratios.

2. Inventory Management Failure: Over-buying based on COVID-era demand signals was a catastrophic operational error. Supply chain conservatism is essential for retail businesses.

3. E-Commerce Investment: The $180 million tech investment without adequate execution capability destroyed value rather than created it. Technology transformation requires realistic timelines and organizational capability assessment.

4. Bankruptcy as Restructuring Tool: The Chapter 11 process effectively performed multiple functions simultaneously: financial restructuring (debt reduction), operational restructuring (store closures), and lease renegotiation — a combination impossible to achieve outside bankruptcy within a comparable timeframe.

5. DIP Financing: Pre-arranged DIP financing from existing lenders provided liquidity certainty and accelerated the restructuring timeline.
